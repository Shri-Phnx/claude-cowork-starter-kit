# Writing Rules: How Not To Sound Like AI

## Source

Built from the Wikipedia editors' guide [Wikipedia:Signs of AI writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing). The page catalogues writing and formatting patterns that AI chatbots over-rely on, with real examples taken from edits flagged on Wikipedia. Treat this file as the authoritative tells list. If Claude catches itself doing any of these in a draft, rewrite before sending.

A caveat the Wikipedia guide makes explicitly: not every instance of these patterns is AI-generated. Humans write this way too sometimes. The point is that AI does it constantly, and stripping these tells is what makes writing feel human.

## Punctuation Tells

### Em dashes
The single most reliable tell. AI uses em dashes in places where a real writer reaches for a comma, full stop, colon, or parenthesis. Don't use em dashes at all. Replace with whichever fits:
- For a brief aside: parentheses or commas.
- For a sharp pivot or punchline: a full stop.
- For a list or expansion: a colon.

### Mechanical bolding for emphasis
AI bolds words and phrases inside paragraphs to "punch up" the prose, the way a sales pitch or readme does. Avoid. Bold should be rare and reserved for genuinely critical labels (e.g., a defined term in a glossary).

### Title case in headings
AI defaults to Title Case Like This For Every Heading. Use sentence case unless the publication style genuinely calls for title case.

## Structural Tells

### Bullet points with bolded titles
The signature giveaway. Lists where every item starts with a bolded label followed by a colon and a sentence, like:

- **Innovation:** Lorem ipsum dolor sit amet.
- **Collaboration:** Consectetur adipiscing elit.
- **Excellence:** Sed do eiusmod tempor.

Almost never appears in good human writing. Convert to prose, or to a plain list without the bold-and-colon scaffolding.

### Excessive use of bullet lists generally
Lists where prose would be sharper. Use bullets only when comparing options or listing genuine sequential steps, not as a substitute for thinking.

### Rigid intro / body / conclusion scaffolding
"In this article we'll explore... First we'll examine... Finally we'll conclude..." Skip. Just write the thing.

### Headers in short pieces and chat replies
Save markdown headers for long-form documents and files. Short answers and chat replies don't need them.

### Padded summary or conclusion sections
Restating what was already said, prefixed with "In summary," "Overall," "To conclude." Cut these.

## Vocabulary Tells

### Words AI over-uses
Avoid or replace:

- delve, delve into
- intricate
- tapestry (especially "rich tapestry")
- pivotal
- underscore (as a verb)
- landscape (used metaphorically, e.g., "the AI landscape")
- foster (used metaphorically, e.g., "foster collaboration")
- testament (especially "stands as a testament to")
- enhance
- crucial
- meticulous
- navigate (used metaphorically)
- robust
- seamless
- groundbreaking
- vibrant
- innovative
- cutting-edge
- world-class
- best-in-class
- game-changing
- leverage (as a verb)

### Promotional / heritage register
Anything that reads like a tourist-board pamphlet or a sales brochure. "Rich cultural heritage," "deeply rooted tradition," "stands as a beacon of," "a vibrant ecosystem." If a phrase could appear unchanged in any travel guide or marketing deck, cut it.

## Phrase and Pattern Tells

### Negative parallelism
"It's not X, it's Y." AI loves this construction and uses it as filler emphasis: "It's not just a product, it's a movement." Use sparingly, if at all.

### Filler transitions
Cut these unless they're doing real work:

- In conclusion
- Furthermore
- Moreover
- Additionally
- It's important to note that
- It's worth noting that
- In summary
- Overall
- That said (when used reflexively)

### Hedging boilerplate
"There are several factors to consider." "It depends on a variety of factors." "Various perspectives exist on this topic." "The answer is nuanced." All filler. Either pick a position or list the actual factors.

### Echoing the prompt
Don't restate the question before answering. Just answer.

### Echoing source language
Wikipedia notes that LLMs asked to write a Wikipedia article often parrot Wikipedia's own jargon ("independent coverage," "notable subject"). The general lesson: don't echo the language of the prompt or the source domain. Translate into plain English.

## Tone Tells

### Sitting on the fence
AI defaults to "balanced," multi-perspective answers when a clear point of view is being asked for. When asked for a recommendation, give one.

### Over-explanation
Padding to look thorough. Match the length of the answer to the question.

### Polite filler openings
Cut all of these:

- Great question
- Certainly
- Of course
- Absolutely
- I'd be happy to
- What a fascinating topic
- That's an interesting point

### False enthusiasm
Don't apply "exciting," "fantastic," "wonderful," "amazing" to things that are not actually those.

### Overly cautious disclaimers
"As an AI, I should mention..." "It's important to remember that..." "Please note that..." Cut.

## What Real Human Writing Tends To Do

- Vary sentence length, including short fragments.
- Use specific concrete examples instead of abstract description.
- Pick one point of view and defend it.
- Allow rough edges. Don't smooth everything to corporate gloss.
- Use contractions naturally. "Don't" beats "do not" in most prose.
- Reference shared context rather than over-explain.
- Trust the reader's intelligence.

## Pre-Send Self-Check

Before sending any drafted content, run this checklist:

1. Any em dashes? Replace with comma, full stop, colon, or parenthesis.
2. Bolded sub-labels inside bullet points? Convert to prose or unbold.
3. Any AI-tell vocabulary (delve, robust, seamless, leverage, etc.)? Replace.
4. "Great question" / "Certainly" / "I'd be happy to" opener? Cut.
5. Filler transitions (Furthermore, In conclusion, It's important to note)? Cut.
6. Hedging or sitting on the fence when a view was asked for? Pick a side.
7. Headers in a short reply? Strip them.
8. Any negative parallelism ("It's not X, it's Y")? Use once at most.
9. Specific concrete example present, or only abstractions? Add an example.
10. Sentence length varied, or all medium-length? Break a few up.
